Reflection

What were your expectations about your internship before you joined?

My main expectation prior to the internship as that my role would be to observe and sit in on project activities. I thought that I would have to take notes and strategy my learnings prior to doing any real development work. I also thought that the fact that I am an undergraduate student that I would not be able to lead any projects or act as the lead software engineer. I was also not aware of the large size of the organisation prior to commencing, and the various directorates and teams that make up the whole company.

What was the reality? How was it different from your expectations?

Over my short time in the organisation, I have work on and led many projects, spanning across multiple directorates and technical stacks. Each involving varied software engineering skills as well as problem and solution analysis. I am also the lead engineer in some projects, which gives me ownership over my solutions and their effectiveness. There are almost two thousand employees here at eHealth that look after building, maintaining and implementing technical project solutions across all public hospitals in New South Wales. This is different to my expectations, as I found my place within the organisation quite quickly.

What lessons were the most important from your internship. Why were they important?

One of the most important lessons I learned is how to ask for and receive feedback, especially since I am at the start of my career. Feedback from senior engineers is golden, as it can help you not only implement a better solution, but become a better engineer. Also, in most cases you are not monitored by a senior engineer, so you responsible to solving your own problems, whether it involves reaching out to a colleague or researching the issue on your own, both of which I have done many times. Additionally, making connections within a large organisation is important, as I have worked across multiple directorates and teams. Having the privilege of connecting with various technical and non-technical people, who have helped me inside and outside of relevant projects. Additionally, making and maintaining connections with people external to the business is important for your personal growth and future aspirations.

After your workplace experience, what would you say your value proposition would be to an employer? How can you demonstrate this?

My value is that I can work in a large organisation effectively, I can liaise and communicate with various people in different roles to achieve a solution, then I can explain the results to relevant technical and non-technical stakeholders. Additionally, working in an agile sprint format as a business analyst as well as a software engineer utilising service now software to operate the sprints, are skills transferrable to many other organisations. I have hands-on experience in software development across multiple tools such as AWS, Snowflake and various coding languages, I have delivered real solutions and troubleshot with these services. Also, my work as a business analyst has demonstrated that I can collect the requirements for a solution and communicate the problem effectively, so that a software engineer, other than I, can build the solution. Which is valuable, as it is rare across software engineering candidates.

How did your internship influence the type of role in which you are interested?

My internship confirms that I am still interested in a software engineering role that is involved with delivering health related solutions. I thought that I would be more interested in clinical solutions, however, I now appreciate the importance of non-clinical solutions and how they can assist with various workflows. I have enjoyed working with DevOps applications such as AWS, and working with technical teams within pipelines, this inspires me to look for a DevOps focused role. However, my work building LLMs with snowflake and copilot studio, influences me to look for an AI engineering role. Both would build upon my skills in those areas. Whatever I am building, I know that I can make a meaningful difference within healthcare and building healthcare related solutions.

Selection Criteria 3.2 - Demonstrated ability to effectively communicate with both other engineers and with stakeholders from different fields

At eHealth NSW, I engineer Amazon Connect contact centres for various NSW health entities. Amazon Connect is a web-based application that handles all call making and taking procedures, this replaces classic desk phones. I actively participate in the business requirements gathering, which includes interviewing the business (NSW health entity) to understand the needs of their service. An example is the various types of calls the business takes in their contact centres. This involves me translating these into IVR (Interactive Voice Response) options, and different queues for the calls to wait in. Then, I design my build and explain the choices in a manner that is easy to understand, so that the business can provide their input. I also confirm my design choices with my engineering colleagues and superiors to ensure that the planned build is best practice. If there are suggested modifications for the design, I implement these and explain the decisions to the non-technical business representatives, so they can maintain an understanding of how the system works.

Selection Criteria 3.3 – The ability to engage with a creative, innovative and proactive environment and 3.5 – The ability to manage your own performance in a professional environment

When working with businesses, their requirements can change once they get a feel of a new system, this usually occurs during UAT (User Acceptance Testing). When working with the Amazon Connect contact centres, I noticed that businesses often want the play prompts (the messages read out to the callers) modified, as these can sound different when simulating calls in the UAT environment. Meaning that flow changes are often required, to ensure that the business is agreeable with the system. I always accommodate for the business by making these changes, and I can suggest creative solutions for when a problem is raised. Additionally, inside the many UAT processes that I have been involved in, I have learnt to monitor my own performance. I do this particularly via the number of defects raised by the testing team. The first contact centre I built had many defects, mostly due to lack of effective testing on my part. I highlighted this an area for improvement, so during the build of the following contact centre, I wrote my own test cases and over-tested my flows. Which involves calling in, listening to the play prompts and ensuring that the correct procedure happens when a call centre agent is offline or engaged in another call. As a result of my thorough testing, the following contact centre reported no build-related defects (there were defects for minor changes to play prompts), showing successful personal performance management.
